<span class="dropcap">Y</span>our auditor opens the Ecoinvent record for the 304 stainless-steel row in your BOM and asks one question: cut-off, APOS, or consequential? You picked the first hit. The database shipped that same steel dataset under three system models, and you took whichever the search returned. Now you have to defend why — and "it was the top result" is not a defense that survives review.

This is not a trick question. It is the question. Ecoinvent ships the same unit-process datasets under three system models — cut-off, APOS (Allocation at the Point of Substitution), and consequential — because they answer three different questions. Choosing the wrong one for your goal and scope does not throw an error. It changes the number, quietly, and when recycling or co-products are in the boundary the shift typically runs to tens of percent — occasionally roughly 2× on recycling-intensive materials.

---

## What the three models actually decide

A system model is a set of modeling rules for handling recycling, co-products, and counterfactuals. The dataset's name does not change. The flows behind it do.

**Cut-off** ("recycled content method"): waste burdens stay with the producer who generated them. A user of recycled material carries the recycling burden but not the burden of the original production. Scrap arrives burden-free at your gate.

**APOS** (Allocation at the Point of Substitution): the burden of original production is allocated between the first product and the recycled material at the point where substitution happens. The recycled input is not free; it carries a share back upstream.

**Consequential**: marginal-effects modeling. It answers *what changes* if you make a decision — which supplier responds at the margin, which co-product gets displaced. It is not a description of an average; it is a model of a counterfactual.

For your stainless-steel row, these are not three flavors of the same answer. Stainless steel is recycling-intensive. Cut-off and APOS treat that scrap content differently by construction, so the cradle-to-gate GWP100 they return differs — often by tens of percent, sometimes around 2×. Consequential asks a question your attributional PCF may not be asking at all.

## The failure mode is silence

> Pick the wrong system model and nothing breaks. The result is just wrong, and it looks exactly as confident as the right one.

There is no warning. The dataset loads, the GWP100 value populates, your model runs, the spreadsheet exports. The mismatch surfaces months later, in review, when the verifier asks which system model your goal and scope called for and whether every background dataset matches it. If three of your forty rows came from cut-off and one came from APOS because the search ranked it first, your study mixes models. That is a finding.

Here is the trap in one illustrative scenario, a China grid-power comparison. The practitioner needs a cradle-to-gate GWP100 figure for the electricity input. GWP100 is an IPCC characterization applied on top of the inventory — every system model can be characterized to it, so that is never the variable that picks the model for you. The variable is which record actually exists for the region. For the `market for electricity, medium voltage, CN` record, only the cut-off variant ships a China-specific dataset; the APOS and consequential variants fall back to a rest-of-world proxy. Take the cut-off number because it is the one with a regional match, without noticing *why* it was the only one on offer, and you have silently committed your whole study to a system model you never chose — picked by data availability, not by your goal and scope.
