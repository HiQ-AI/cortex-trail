<span class="dropcap">T</span>he row that stopped me was the third one down. An industrial IoT controller, maybe forty parts in the bill of materials, and most of them were the kind of thing you can match in your sleep: a 304 stainless bracket, an FR-4 board, a couple of standard connectors. Then row three. Under "material," one word: 「高分子」. Polymer. The supplier had, in a later revision, generously upgraded this to 「树脂」 — resin. Two non-standard names, no grade, no CAS number, no datasheet. A mass figure, and nothing else to hang it on.

I have been doing this long enough to know what happens next. You email the supplier. The supplier forwards it to someone who forwards it to procurement, and three weeks later you get back a PDF that says, in effect, "polymer." Meanwhile the PCF is due, and you have a choice that every practitioner has made and most of us would rather not put in writing: pick a proxy and move on.

## A guess is not a substitution

The honest problem with row three is not that I lacked a number. I could have typed "generic plastic, GWP100 around 3 kg CO2e/kg" and nobody would have blinked at the draft. The problem is what that number does later. A proxy substitution is the first thing an auditor circles. Not because it is wrong, but because it is undefended — a value with no line back to a decision. If I cannot say *why this resin and not that one*, the row is a liability dressed up as data.

So this time I did not guess. I handed row three to Cortex and watched what it did with almost nothing.

## Reading the row it was not given

What Cortex had to work with was not the word 「树脂」 in isolation. It had the surrounding inventory — the process context, the function of the part, the other materials in the same assembly — and it read across all of it. From that, it proposed a specific material: PAM, polyacrylamide, at above 95% confidence. Not "a plastic." A named polymer, inferred from what the rest of the BOM implied about what this row had to be.

That is the part I want to be precise about, because the confidence number is doing real work here and it would be easy to oversell. Cortex did not declare the answer. It proposed PAM as the most likely identity and then went looking for literature to verify the inference — treating its own high-confidence read as a hypothesis to check, not a verdict to file. The clarification was on the screen: here is the material I think this is, here is why, here is what I am about to confirm it against.

## When the search came back broken

Then the interesting failure. The first database pass returned malformed record links — entries that pointed nowhere, the retrieval equivalent of a dead reference. The thing I have watched lesser tools do at exactly this moment is hand you the broken result anyway, ranked and confident, a tidy number sitting on top of garbage.

Cortex caught it. It detected that the records were bad and re-queried — a self-correcting search rather than a single shot it was willing to defend regardless of what came back. This is the quiet competence that matters: not that the first search was perfect, but that the engine noticed it was not, and said so, and went again. A clean failure that announces itself beats a dirty success every time.

> The first answer was wrong. What I trust is that the engine knew it was wrong.

## Top-k, and then it stopped

The re-query returned what I actually needed: not one number, but several proxy candidates for the PAM row, each carried across the fourteen databases Cortex searches — HiQLCD, Ecoinvent, EF, CarbonMinds, and others — and each scored on the five DQI dimensions, Temporal, Geographic, Technology, Completeness, Reliability. Top-k, not top-1. I could see the spread, which means I could see the risk.

And then Cortex did the thing that is the whole reason I reach for it on a row like this. It did not silently drop the closest proxy into the cell and call the row done. A proxy substitution — the closest match that is not an exact fit — is exactly where automation would break an audit, so the decision came back to me, with an expert recommendation for the next step attached. Which candidate, on what grounds, with the geographic and technology deltas named. The practitioner decides; the choice is recorded in the reasoning chain.

That is the difference between a number and a defensible row. The proxy I chose is still a proxy. But now there is a line from 「树脂」 to PAM to the candidate I picked to the reason I picked it — and when the auditor circles row three, I have a trail to walk them back along, not a shrug.

## What I keep coming back to

I did not save three weeks because the supplier still owes me a real datasheet, and a confirmed material always beats an inferred one. What changed is that I no longer had to choose between an undefended guess and a stalled filing. Cortex read a row it was barely given, proposed a specific material, checked itself when the search came back broken, and then — at the one point where guessing would have cost me later — stopped and asked.

If you have a BOM with a row like that on it, the kind you have been quietly dreading, ask Cortex about it. Bring the worst row you have.
