<span class="dropcap">P</span>ull the GWP100 factor for one kWh of Chinese solar PV from Ecoinvent v3.12.0, cut-off — applying IPCC GWP100 characterization to that inventory — and you get 0.080 kg CO2-eq. Pull the same kWh from HiQLCD v1.4.0, the China LCI database, and you get 0.029. The two numbers differ by 176%. That gap is now in your export carbon label, your product carbon footprint, your carbon-market price. It is not a rounding question. It decides whether your product reads as clean or dirty at the border.

Same kWh. Same horizon. Same characterization. The number you ship depends entirely on which database your background data came from. Here is why the two disagree, where the disagreement is a representativeness problem and where it is a boundary choice, and what your tooling should do when it sees a spread that wide.

## The spread, by source

Five China electricity sources, GWP100, kg CO2-eq per kWh, HiQLCD v1.4.0 against Ecoinvent v3.12.0 cut-off:

- **Solar PV** — 0.029 vs 0.080. They disagree by 176%.
- **Wind, onshore** — 0.009 vs 0.031. They disagree by 244%.
- **Coal thermal** — 1.033 vs 1.210. Within 17%.
- **Run-of-river hydro** — 0.012 vs 0.005. HiQLCD higher.
- **Nuclear PWR** — 0.006 vs 0.008. Close.

Read the pattern before you read the cause. The two databases agree closely on nuclear. They sit within a coal plant's noise on thermal. They invert on hydro. They diverge by nearly 3x on exactly the two sources your low-carbon story leans on: solar and wind. (The HiQLCD solar and wind figures assume a utility-scale capacity factor with infrastructure fully amortized over generation; the low anchors follow from those boundary choices, which a verifier is entitled to ask you to state.)

## Where the gap comes from

For several key manufacturing sub-processes — wafer and cell production energy, certain turbine components — specific Ecoinvent records still lean on European or global average inventories rather than China-built data, so the modeled energy intensity does not reflect China's actual cell-line efficiency. This is not a claim that China data does not exist; it is a statement about the geographic and technology representativeness of the particular records in play. China manufactures most of the world's PV cells and a large share of its turbines, at an efficiency a transplanted inventory does not see. On exactly the two technologies where China's industrial scale matters most, the two databases part company on the manufacturing burden.

The electricity mix underneath compounds it. Ecoinvent represents China electricity through regional market mixes dominated by hard-coal and lignite generation, with several generation technologies modeled at global or rest-of-world average where China-specific inventories are absent. HiQLCD resolves the same picture from a China-built energy inventory across all 31 provinces at provincial precision. When the grid that powers the factory leans on a coal-weighted proxy, the manufacturing footprint inherits that proxy's carbon, and the difference in the background grid stacks on the difference already in the manufacturing inventory.

> Two credible databases, looking at the same Chinese kWh, hand you two different numbers. The spread is the finding, not the footnote.

Not every gap traces to manufacturing representativeness. On run-of-river hydro, HiQLCD reads *higher* — 0.012 against 0.005 — because HiQLCD amortizes the civil works (the diversion weir, intake, penstock, and powerhouse — concrete and steel) over generation at a low capacity factor, and Ecoinvent's boundary draws the line differently. That is an infrastructure-amortization boundary disagreement, not an accuracy defect: a question about what counts as part of generating the electricity. Nuclear (0.006 vs 0.008) and coal (1.033 vs 1.210) sit close; the residual difference is consistent with known boundary differences in the uranium fuel cycle on one and with plant-to-plant variability on the other. Knowing which kind of gap you are looking at is the whole job. One you correct. One you document.

## The averaging trap

Most automated factor-matching does the convenient thing with a 0.029-versus-0.080 spread: it picks one, or it splits the difference. Either move buries the most important fact about your solar row — that two credible databases disagree about it by 176% — under a single tidy number your auditor cannot reconstruct. Take a naive arithmetic average and you get 0.054, a figure no database holds and no one chose. Pick silently and you have made a methodology decision your reasoning chain never records.

This is the failure mode the Audit Trail exists to name. The spread *is* the signal. Hiding it is the error.
