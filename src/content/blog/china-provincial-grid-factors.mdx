Your study lists one input for both: "China electricity." Your verifier opens the file, finds a national-average grid factor behind that input, and asks the question you didn't: which province? That one substitution can move the cradle-to-gate carbon footprint more than any other line in the bill of materials — and it is the line most spreadsheets get wrong by default.

China is not one grid. A national average is the weighted blend of the two. For a product made in one known province, that blend is not a representativeness choice you can defend. It is the wrong number for that goal and scope, wearing the costume of a defensible one.

## What a national average hides

A grid factor is the GWP100 result for one kWh of supplied electricity, and it is geography all the way down. The northern coal grids and the south-western hydro grids sit at opposite ends of the same country's spectrum. Average them and you get a figure that describes no real production site — least of all the one your supplier actually operates.

The damage is structural, not cosmetic. Electricity is rarely a footnote in a metals or chemicals product system; it is often the dominant contributor to cradle-to-gate GWP. So the province you pick for "China electricity" can swing the result more than the choice of any raw material. A verifier who knows the field knows this. Your auditor's first question about a China-sited product is almost never about the steel. It is: which grid, and what year.

Many international databases cannot answer that question. They offer a China national average, or a generic electricity dataset transplanted from another region and relabeled. Either way, the geographic provenance the verifier needs is gone before the calculation begins. You inherit a number you cannot defend on its own terms.

None of this makes a national average useless. For a study whose goal and scope are national — mixed-sourcing, unspecified site, country-level reporting — the blend is the right and defensible figure. The error is using it for a single-site product whose province you already know.

## Geographic is a DQI dimension, not a rounding error

The Data Quality Indicator — built on the same Pedigree-Matrix lineage practitioners already know — scores every candidate dataset across five dimensions, in this order: Temporal, Geographic, Technology, Completeness, Reliability. Geographic measures how well a dataset's region matches the process you are studying. A national average applied to a single-province product scores poorly on exactly that dimension — and a national average is precisely the substitution that throws the dimension away while leaving the rest of the file looking complete.

That is the trap. The number looks finished. The Temporal field has a year, the GWP column has a value, the source has a URL. Nothing on the surface flags that the Geographic match is weak. The mismatch only surfaces when a verifier walks the trail back — or when a tool scores Geographic explicitly and shows you the gap before you file.

> For a single-province product whose site you know, a national average is not a smaller error than a wrong material. It is often the largest one in the file.
